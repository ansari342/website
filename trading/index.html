    Lab notes: ideas tested and rejected

    Gap Fill (buy funds that open sharply lower, sell the same day). It won about 58–62% of trades, but its average result was slightly negative after a 0.05% trading cost. Kept in the list above so you can see why a high win rate alone isn't enough.

    Overnight hold (buy at the close, sell at the next open). The raw data shows unusually strong overnight gains in these funds, but the effect disappears once realistic costs are added, and it is much weaker in the second half of the data. That pattern usually comes from the gap between buying and selling prices in thinly traded funds, so it's very unlikely to be capturable with real orders.

    Turn of the month (hold over the last and first days of each month). It was positive, but too weak (about 2–4% a year) to justify the trading.

    01 · How it trades

    Every morning, a model estimates each stock's chance that a trade bought at the open ends with a profit after costs. It buys only when that chance clears your bar. It sells at the next open after the stock closes above its 5-day average, after a maximum number of days, or at a protective stop. Long only: a likely loser means staying in cash.

    Lab notes

    Same-day version. The first version sold every position at the close and bought when the chance of a same-day gain was 55% or more. Across 52 sampled weeks it averaged −0.25% a week, and when it said "57%" the stock rose only 48% of the time.

    Intraday momentum (if a stock is up in its first hour, buy for the last half hour). Tested on 2 years of hourly prices for the same 10 stocks and funds, about 1,600 stock-days. It ran the wrong way: after an up first hour, the last half hour averaged −0.045%. Buying after a down first hour averaged +0.012%, far below a round-trip trading cost.

    Stocks picked with hindsight. NVIDIA, ASML and the other stocks in the list were chosen knowing they did well, which flatters every strategy that trades them, including Dip Buyer's result here.

    Hill climbing · tuned on the past, tested on the future

    Each strategy's settings were tuned by hill climbing: change one setting a step at a time, keep any change that scores better, and repeat until nothing improves (with three random restarts). The score is yearly return divided by the worst drop, with drops under 10% counted as 10%, and at least 20 trades.

    Tuning used only . The chosen settings were then run on , which the search never saw. Only the test column tells you what to expect. Settings that shine in tuning and fade in testing were overfit.
